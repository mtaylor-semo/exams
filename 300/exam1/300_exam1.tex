\question
The following mutation rates were found in yeast (\textit{Saccharomyces cerevisiae}). Rates are per base pair per generation.

\begin{parts}
\part[5]
{\lining Zhu et al.~(2014) estimated that the average genome-wide mutation rate was 1.67 $\times$ 10\textsuperscript{-10}. 75\% of the yeast genome is coding and 25\% is non-coding. Would you predict the mutation rate in the non-coding region to be higher or lower than the genome-wide rate? Tell why.}

\AnswerBox{0.25\textheight}{Predict non-coding rate to be higher. Most of their \textsc{dna} is coding so the overall rate is probably lower because detrimental mutations removed by natural selection. This would not affect non-coding regions, leading to higher average mutation rate.}


\part[5]
Lang and Murray~(2008) compared mutation rates for two genes. Gene \textit{\textsc{ura3}} had a mutation rate of {\lining 3.8 $\times$ 10\textsuperscript{-10}}. Gene \textit{\textsc{can1}} had a mutation rate of {\lining 6.4 $\times$ 10\textsuperscript{-10}}. Provide a reason why these genes have different mutation rates and how this difference provides support for the Neutral Theory.

\AnswerBox{0.25\textheight}{\textsc{ura3} might be more conserved so more mutations would be deleterious compared to \textsc{can1}. Neutral theory says that natural selection removes detrimental mutations so \textsc{ura3} would appear to have lower mutation rate.}
\end{parts}

\question[5]
Tell whether population size affects the rate of haplotype substitution by genetic drift in a population. Demonstrate this for population sizes of 50 and 100 and a neutral mutation rate of 0.05 in each population. 

\AnswerBox{0.1\textheight}{Haplotype substitution is independent of population size and occurs at the neutral mutation rate. 
$$2 \cdot 50\cdot 0.05 \times \frac{1}{2\cdot 50} = 0.05.$$
$$2 \cdot 500\cdot 0.05 \times \frac{1}{2\cdot 500} = 0.05.$$}


\question[15]
Based on the principles for mutation rate, genetic drift, and natural selection, do you expect the rate of \emph{adaptive} evolution to occur more rapidly in small or large populations, or at about the same rate regardless of population size? \emph{Explain your reasoning.} Be sure to include the roles of mutation rate, genetic drift, and selection in your answers. Disregard other assumptions of Hardy-Weinberg.

\begin{AnswerPage}{0.4\textheight}
	
	Adaptive evolution will occur more rapidly in large populations. Although mutation rate is independent of population size, more mutations will occur in larger populations. For any mutation that is even slightly beneficial, it has a much better chance of becoming fixed in the population if the strength of selection $(s)$ is much larger than $1/4N_e$. That means $s$ can be very small in large populations. Simulations showed that even weakly favored mutations can increase rapidly in frequency.  In small populations, few mutations will occur so won't get beneficial mutations as often. Unless there is stronger strength of selection to overcome the effects of drift, the new mutation is more likely to be lost due to drift.
	
\end{AnswerPage}

\question[10]
Calculate whether genetic drift or natural selection is more likely to be the \textit{primary} cause of allele frequency change in a population of $N_e = 4000$, if the fitness of the three genotypes is $A_1A_1 = 0.999$, $A_1A_2 = 0.999$, and $A_2A_2 = 1.0$. Show your math and explain your reasoning. 

	\begin{AnswerPage}{0.25\textheight}
	\textit{s} = 1 - 0.999 = 0.001. (2 points)\\
	1/2N\textsubscript{e} = 1 / (2\,\times\,4000) = 1/8000 = 0.000125. (2 points) \vspace{\baselineskip}
	
	$s$ is about 10 times greater than $1/2N_e$. When $s$ is much greater than $1/2N_e$, then selection will be the primary cause of allele frequency change in the population. (4 points)
	\end{AnswerPage}

\question[5]
The values of \textit{w} for three genotypes in a population are $B_1B_1$ = 0.98, $B_1B_2$ = 1.0, and $B_2B_2$ = 0.96. Name the mode of selection that explains how the phenotype would change in this population. Tell how you know.

\AnswerBox{0.2\textheight}{%
Stablilizing selection. Heterozygotes have the greatest fitness (heterozygote advantage) so the population will converge on the intermediate phenotype.
}
