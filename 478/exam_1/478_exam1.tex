\question[15]
Sharks and tunas both cruise the open seas.  Despite this commonality, these two fishes have very different body forms.  Paul Webb, on pages 80--81 of his Scientific American article, “Form and Function in Fish Swimming,” explains why these two fishes differ so greatly.  Describe one physiological, one structural and one ecological difference between sharks and tunas, and then explain how these three differences relate to each other to explain the overall morphological differences between sharks and tunas.
	\begin{solution}
	Physiological: Sharks are energy conservative. They do not swim as fast or as often as tunas. They are not endothermic/heterothermic so they do not require as much energy input. Tunas are “energy speculators” meaning they burn lots of energy to be endothermic/heterothermic. They swim fast and continuously.
	
	Structural: Sharks have cartilaginous skeletons, so they cannot generate the propulsive forces necessary to maintain high speed. They thus swim more slowly. Tunas have rigid, body skeleton so they can generate strong propulsive forces necessary to sustain high speed swimming.
	
	Ecological: Sharks are opportunistic predators. They are not constantly searching for prey because they do not have the high energy demands. Tuna must search constantly for prey to meet the high energy needs.
	\end{solution}

\question[15]
Diagram and explain how a physoclistous bladder is inflated and emptied. Be sure to include all of the key structures and physiological processes.  Indicate proper direction of flow for all molecules involved. Use representative values for pH and oxygen concentration to aid your explanation.

\begin{solution}
(15 points) Incoming blood as high pH and high O$_2$ bound to hemoglobin. Because O$_2$ is bound to hemoglobin, the partial pressure of free O$_2$ in the blood is low. Gas gland takes up glucose from the incoming blood, produces lactic acid which diffuses into bloodstream. Lactic acid lowers pH, causing Root Effect. Root effect decreases O$_2$ capacity, so hemoglobin releases O$_2$. Some of the O$_2$ diffuses into gas bladder. Some of the O$_2$ and lactic acid enters the outgoing capillary. Outgoing blood contains higher concentrations of lactic acid and free O$_2$, so it diffuses into incoming blood via countercurrent exchange. This allows high pressures of O$_2$ to build up in rete mirabile and diffuse into gas bladder.

(5 points) To empty, the oval is a blood network that can press against the gas bladder. A \emph{contracted} sphincter allows the oval to contact the bladder and gasses diffuse out of the bladder into the blood stream. A \emph{relaxed} sphincter prevents contact of the oval with the bladder, preventing diffusion of the gasses out of the bladder.
\end{solution}

\question[15]
Explain how stenohaline freshwater and saltwater bony fishes maintain osmotic homeostasis.  Explain for water and for relevant ions (e.g., Na$^+$, Cl$^-$, K$^+$, Ca$^{2+}$).  Include for each when diffusion is operating and when active transport is required, and the primary regions for entry and exit of water and ions.  Use illustrations to enhance your explanation.  Do not include \emph{elasmobranchs or hagfishes}.

\begin{solution}
(10 points) Freshwater fishes are hypertonic relative to their environment. Therefore, water diffuses in passively. Salts diffuse out of gills passively. They produce a dilute urine to get rid of excess water. Kidneys resorb divalent ions. They use beta chloride cells to actively transport salts from the water into the fish. Salts also obtained by their food. 

(10 points) Saltwater fishes are hypotonic relative to their environment. THerefore, marine fishes lose water passively and gain salts passively. Fishes drink sea water to replace lost water. They actively remove monovalent ions by alpha chloride cells in the gills. They actively remove divalent ions via the kidneys, released in a concentrated urine to help retain water.
\end{solution}
